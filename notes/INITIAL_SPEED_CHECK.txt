Initial speed check: everything we tried before the final models (serve speed only)

Based on the basketball pose paper (Williams et al.) we first asked whether the serving arm (shoulder, elbow, wrist)
predicts serve speed as well as the full 17-joint skeleton. We use the 2024 US Open serve dataset (keypoints and
metadata only), which lives in tennis_serve_data/. This note lists what we checked and what we found; the numbers are in
our results files (not in the repo).

1. Preparing the data
Positions are taken relative to the hips and smoothed with a Butterworth low-pass filter (8 Hz; 6 and 12 Hz were also
tried). We tried to detect the serving arm from the pose, but the left and right wrists are almost the same point in this
data, so none of our rules worked. Instead we use a player handedness table (src/handedness.py). Only two players are
listed as left-handed, and the rest were not checked one by one.

2. First models (code now in src/archive/initial_speed_check/)
- A Random Forest on per-joint summary features (distance moved, peak speed, time of the peak, mean speed,
  accelerations) for the serving arm and for all joints, against a baseline from gender, serve number and height. It
  reproduced the baseline and then tried ridge, residual Random Forest and boosting models with repeated player-grouped
  cross-validation and player-level bootstrap intervals, plus a control with shuffled pose features, which did about as
  well as the real ones.
- A metadata-only notebook (gender + serve number + height) to see how much of the speed the baseline already explains.
- A small transformer on the raw positions, first on raw and then on size-scaled positions, always split by player (this early script is no longer kept).
None of these found a useful link between the joints and serve speed beyond the baseline, for 3 joints or for 17 joints.
We also wrote frame-skipping scripts that drop every second frame, and sweep over several steps, to see whether the
frame rate matters for speed prediction; their results are not summarised here.

3. Related checks
Variation within one player, first vs second serve, and serve width and depth also found nothing, while the same features
could identify the player.

4. Pipeline check (src/pipeline_check.py)
To see whether the speed result comes from the data or from our pipeline, we checked: the size and units of the motion;
whether the wrist peak falls at a consistent point in the clip; whether faster arms go with faster serves within one
player and serve number; the smoothing cutoff (6, 8, 12 Hz); size-scaled vs raw features; and not relying on the
handedness table at all. The result held up in every case, so it looks like a limit of the data and not of our code.

5. Speed on a single split (src/random_forest/speed_pooled.py, speed_stratified.py)
A separate Random Forest pipeline with one 80/20 split by player (seed 42): pooled over all serves, and within each of
the four groups female/male x first/second serve, with and without height in the baseline. It reaches the same conclusion:
pose does not add to the metadata baseline for speed, for the arm or for the body.

6. What came next
Because speed was not predictable from pose, we widened the question to four targets (gender, player identity, serve
number, serve speed) and asked which of them the serving arm carries and which need the full body. See HYPOTHESIS.txt
for the question and the predictions.

Limits: the transformer in the first attempt was a single small setup with no tuning, and the serve speeds come from a
match feed that we could not check against the video.
