. Serve Speed
The serve-speed analysis was developed in three stages.
1.1 Initial experiment: the influence of metadata
The first experiment used the complete dataset to investigate the relationship between serve speed, player metadata and body-pose information.

The models included:
- A mean-speed baseline.
- Linear regression models using gender, serve number and height.
- Random Forest models using metadata and pose features.
- Pose-only Random Forest models, using either the serving arm or the full body.
The results showed that gender and serve number have a strong influence on serve speed. A model using these two variables achieved an MAE of approximately 10.64 km/h, compared with 18.57 km/h for the mean baseline.

Adding height to the linear regression model did not produce a meaningful improvement. Similarly, pose-only models performed close to the mean baseline:
- Arm-only: MAE of 18.69 km/h.
- Body-only: MAE of 18.11 km/h.
Adding pose features to metadata also did not produce a convincing improvement.

These findings raised an important question: were the models mainly learning differences between player groups and serve types, rather than extracting useful biomechanical information from the poses?
1.2 Second experiment: analysing four subgroups separately
To investigate this further, the dataset was divided into four groups:
- Female first serves.
- Female second serves.
- Male first serves.
- Male second serves.
Within each group, gender and serve number are fixed. This makes it possible to investigate whether pose information and height help explain speed differences within the same category of serve.

Four models were compared:
- Mean baseline.
- Arm-only Random Forest.
- Body-only Random Forest.
- Height-only Random Forest.
Height showed its clearest predictive contribution for male first serves. Its MAE was 13.64 km/h, compared with 17.42 km/h for the mean baseline. The bootstrap confidence interval also supported an improvement for this group.

For the other three groups, height did not show a statistically clear improvement over the mean baseline.

This suggests that height can retain useful predictive information even after controlling for gender and serve number, although the effect is not equally strong across groups.
1.3 Main biomechanical comparison: arm, body or mean
The main objective was to determine whether the serving arm or the entire body provides useful information for predicting serve speed.

The results did not show a consistent advantage for either pose representation over the mean baseline.

The arm-only model did not outperform the mean baseline in any of the four groups. The body-only model also did not provide a consistent improvement.

The direct comparison between arm and body did not reveal a general advantage for using the full body. The only statistically clear difference was found for male second serves, where the arm-only model performed better than the body-only model.

Overall, the experiments suggest that the pose features used here have limited predictive value for serve speed, particularly when compared with the information provided by metadata and height.

2. Gender Classification
The gender experiment investigated whether body-pose features can distinguish between male and female players.

Different feature representations were compared, using either the serving arm or the entire body:
- Shape features, describing joint positions.
- Motion features, describing how joints move during the serve.
- Scaled motion features.
- Combinations of shape and motion.
The experiment included 5,966 serves from 109 players and used repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds).

The results showed that pose information can provide useful information for gender classification. The best result was obtained using full-body shape combined with scaled motion, achieving a balanced accuracy of 77.5%. Full-body shape alone achieved 75.1%, while arm shape alone achieved only 47.1%.

However, one particularly important finding is that serving-arm motion alone performs almost as well as the best full-body representation. Arm motion achieved a balanced accuracy of 76.2%, and arm scaled motion achieved 75.5%. The difference between arm motion and the best full-body model was only 1.3 percentage points.

The bootstrap comparisons support this observation:
- Full-body shape clearly outperformed arm shape (difference of 28.1 percentage points; 95% CI: 20.5–35.3).
- Full-body motion and arm motion performed similarly (difference of -0.2 percentage points; 95% CI: -2.6 to 1.9).
- Full-body scaled motion and arm scaled motion also showed no significant difference (difference of 1.2 percentage points; 95% CI: -1.1 to 3.5).
Another important observation is that adding motion to arm shape substantially improved performance: balanced accuracy increased from 47.1% to 75.9% when motion features were added. In contrast, adding motion to full-body shape did not produce a statistically clear improvement.

Overall, the results suggest that gender-related information is strongly represented in body shape, but movement patterns from the serving arm alone are already highly informative. Using the entire body does not provide a clear additional advantage over arm motion for this classification task.
3. Player Identity Classification
The identity experiment investigated whether pose features can distinguish between individual players.

In this experiment, each player represents a separate classification class. Different arm and full-body feature representations were compared, as in the gender experiment.

A match-level split was used. This means that serves from a match were kept together rather than being distributed between training and test data.

An important limitation was that a player needed to appear in at least two different matches to be included in the identity evaluation. This was necessary to ensure that the model was tested on matches it had not seen during training.

As a result:
- The original dataset contained 109 players and 5,966 serves.
- 46 players, with 4,264 serves, were eligible for the identity experiment.
- 63 players were excluded because they appeared in only one match.
This exclusion applies only to the identity experiment.

The results showed that full-body pose features were substantially more informative than arm-only features.

The best result was obtained with full-body shape and motion, achieving a balanced accuracy of 45.6%. Full-body shape alone achieved 45.2%, while arm-only shape achieved just 9.6%.

Although identifying individual players remains a challenging task, the results indicate that information useful for distinguishing players is distributed across the body rather than being limited to the serving arm.
## 4. Serve Number Classification

The final experiment investigated whether pose features can distinguish between first and second serves.

Different feature representations were tested, including:

- Arm and full-body shape.
- Arm and full-body motion.
- Scaled motion.
- Combinations of shape and motion.

In addition to pose-only models, models combining pose features with metadata (gender and height) were evaluated.

The dataset contained 5,966 serves from 109 players:

- 4,202 first serves (70.4%).
- 1,764 second serves (29.6%).

The models were evaluated using repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds).

The results were consistently close to **50% balanced accuracy**, which corresponds to chance-level performance for this binary classification task. Although ordinary accuracy was approximately 70%, this is explained by the imbalance between first and second serves: a model predicting the majority class already achieves 70.4% accuracy.

Neither pose-only models nor models combining pose features with metadata produced a meaningful improvement over the baselines. The best balanced accuracy was only 50.1%, achieved by the metadata model and some body-shape representations.

Some paired bootstrap comparisons detected statistically significant differences, but these were extremely small (approximately 0.1–0.2 percentage points) and have no meaningful practical impact.

Overall, the results suggest that **the pose representations tested in this experiment do not contain useful information for reliably distinguishing first and second serves**.&#x20;
5. General Conclusions
The experiments show that the usefulness of body-pose information depends strongly on the target being investigated.
- Serve speed: Gender and serve number explain a substantial amount of the variation. Pose-only models provide little predictive improvement over the mean baseline. Height is particularly informative for male first serves.
- Gender: Full-body shape and serving-arm motion both provide useful classification information. In particular, arm motion achieves performance comparable to the best full-body representation.
- Player identity: Full-body features are considerably more informative than arm-only features, suggesting that individual characteristics are distributed throughout the body.
- Serve number: The tested pose representations do not meaningfully distinguish first and second serves.
An important overall observation is that the full body is not always more informative than the serving arm. Its usefulness depends on what we are trying to predict. Full-body information is particularly valuable for gender shape and player identity, while serving-arm motion alone is already highly informative for gender classification. Neither representation provides a consistent advantage for serve-speed prediction.

6. Evaluation and Methodological Notes
The experiments used different evaluation strategies depending on the target:
- The initial serve-speed experiment used a player-level 80/20 train/test split.
- The four-subgroup serve-speed experiment used player-level splits within each gender, keeping the same player assignment across first and second serves.
- Gender and serve-number classification used repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds).
- Player identity used repeated match-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds).
For the classification experiments, performance was assessed using accuracy, balanced accuracy and macro F1-score. For serve-speed prediction, MAE, RMSE and R² were used.

Bootstrap confidence intervals were also calculated for model comparisons. These help assess whether observed differences are supported by the evaluation data. A confidence interval containing zero should be interpreted as insufficient evidence of a clear difference, rather than proof that two models are equivalent.

The serve-speed experiments use fixed holdout splits, whereas the classification experiments use repeated cross-validation. Their results should therefore be interpreted with these differences in evaluation design in mind.
