1. Serve Speed

The serve-speed analysis was developed in two stages. Both use the same player-level cross-validation as the other targets (5 folds, repeated 3 times), so no player appears in both training and test.

1.1 Stage 1: what explains serve speed?

Models compared, on all 5,966 serves:

A mean-speed baseline.
Linear regression on metadata, added step by step: gender, then serve number, then height.
Random Forest on pose only (serving arm or full body).
Random Forest on gender + serve number, with and without pose.

Results (MAE, lower is better):

Mean baseline: 20.0 km/h.
Gender: 17.7 km/h.
Gender + serve number: 11.7 km/h.
Gender + serve number + height: 11.1 km/h. Height improves the error by 0.6 km/h (95% CI 0.2–1.0).

Gender and serve number explain most of the variation. Height adds a small but clear improvement.

Pose-only models stay close to the mean baseline:

Arm only: 19.7 km/h (no clear improvement over the mean).
Body only: 19.4 km/h (0.6 km/h better than the mean). This small gain most likely reflects gender information carried by the pose, not speed information.

Adding pose to gender + serve number makes the predictions slightly worse:

Arm: +0.3 km/h (95% CI 0.2–0.4).
Body: +0.2 km/h (95% CI 0.1–0.3).

1.2 Stage 2: four subgroups

To remove the effect of gender and serve number completely, the serves were divided into four groups:

Female first serves (1,896 serves, 54 players).
Female second serves (791 serves, 52 players).
Male first serves (2,306 serves, 55 players).
Male second serves (973 serves, 53 players).

Within each group, models were trained separately and compared with the subgroup mean:

Mean baseline.
Height only.
Arm only.
Body only.

Results:

Height helps only for male first serves: 0.7 km/h better than the mean (95% CI 0.1–1.4). In the other three groups it gives no clear improvement.
Arm and body never improve on the subgroup mean. They are slightly but significantly worse in 5 of 8 cases and no different in the rest. With no speed signal in the features, the model fits noise.
Arm and body show no clear difference from each other in any group.

Overall, the pose features carry no information about serve speed beyond gender, serve number and height.

2. Gender Classification

The gender experiment investigated whether pose can distinguish male and female players, and whether the serving arm is enough.

Feature representations, for the serving arm (3 joints) and the full body (17 joints):

Shape: bone lengths, i.e. body proportions.
Motion: speed, acceleration, path length and timing of each joint.
Scaled motion: motion divided by a limb length.
Combinations of shape and motion.

Data: 5,966 serves from 109 players, repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds). Metrics: balanced accuracy (chance = 50%) and AUC (chance = 0.5).

Results (balanced accuracy / AUC):

Arm shape: 58.5% / 0.61
Body shape: 76.2% / 0.84
Arm motion: 74.3% / 0.83
Body motion: 76.5% / 0.85
Arm shape + motion: 75.2% / 0.83
Body shape + motion: 79.7% / 0.88
Best: body shape + scaled motion: 81.0% / 0.88

Main findings:

The serving arm carries most of the gender information: about 75% with motion.
The full body is better, but only by a small margin: +4.6 percentage points for shape + motion (95% CI 1.9–7.3).
For the arm, movement matters much more than proportions: arm shape alone reaches only 58.5%, while adding motion raises it to 75.2% (+16.6 points).
For the body, proportions alone already reach 76.2%. Adding motion gives no clear improvement.

Overall, gender is clearly carried by the pose. The serving arm alone is already highly informative, and the full body adds a little on top.

3. Player Identity Classification

The identity experiment investigated whether pose can tell individual players apart. Each player is a separate class.

A match-level split was used: all serves from a match stay together, so the model must recognise a player in a match it has never seen. A player needed at least two matches to be included:

The dataset contains 109 players and 5,966 serves.
46 players with 4,264 serves were eligible for this experiment.
63 players were excluded because they appear in only one match.

Metrics: balanced accuracy and top-1 accuracy. Chance is 1/46 ≈ 2.2%.

Results (balanced accuracy / top-1 accuracy):

Arm shape: 8.0% / 13.8%
Body shape: 39.8% / 53.5%
Arm motion: 25.0% / 37.9%
Body motion: 34.7% / 49.5%
Arm shape + motion: 28.5% / 42.3%
Body shape + motion: 41.6% / 57.0%

Main findings:

The full body is clearly more informative than the arm: +13.1 points for shape + motion (95% CI 9.7–16.6).
Most of the identity information is in body proportions: body shape alone reaches 39.8%, and adding motion gives no clear improvement.
For the arm, it is the movement that matters: arm proportions alone are close to chance (8.0%), while arm motion reaches 25.0%.
Recognition varies a lot from player to player (from 0% to 97%, median 40%). This makes the confidence intervals of the individual models wide, but the comparison between arm and body is precise, because both are tested on the same players.

Overall, the information needed to tell players apart is spread across the body, mainly in its proportions.

4. Serve Number Classification

The final experiment investigated whether pose can distinguish first and second serves.

Data: 5,966 serves from 109 players:

4,202 first serves (70.4%).
1,764 second serves (29.6%).

Repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds). Because the classes are imbalanced, the Random Forest was trained with class weighting. Without it, the model predicts “first serve” for almost every serve. Metrics: balanced accuracy and AUC. Models with pose only, and with pose + metadata (gender and height), were compared with a metadata-only baseline.

Results:

All pose models reach a balanced accuracy of 50–51% and an AUC of at most 0.52.
Metadata only (gender + height): AUC 0.50.
Pose + metadata does not improve on metadata alone.
Arm and body show no difference.

Plain accuracy is misleading here: always predicting “first serve” already gives 70.4%.

Overall, the pose representations tested here do not distinguish first and second serves. AUC 0.52 means the model ranks a random second serve above a random first serve only 52% of the time, compared with 50% by chance.

5. General Conclusions

How useful the pose is depends strongly on the target.

Gender: carried mostly by the serving arm (about 75%); the full body adds a little (about 80%).
Player identity: the full body is clearly needed (42% vs 28%), mostly through body proportions.
Serve number: the pose does not distinguish first and second serves.
Serve speed: gender and serve number explain most of it, and height adds a little. The pose adds nothing.

In short, traits that stay the same for a player (gender, identity) are present in the skeleton, while serve-to-serve differences (speed, first vs second serve) are not. The full body is not always more informative than the serving arm: it matters for identity, adds only a little for gender, and makes no difference for speed or serve number.

These conclusions agree with the transformer experiments on all four targets.

6. Evaluation and Methodological Notes

Joint order and serving arm:

The 3D keypoints are in Human3.6M joint order (the output of MotionBERT), not COCO as the dataset description suggests. All results here use the correct H36M indices.
The serving arm is chosen per serve as the side whose wrist reaches the higher peak speed. The left/right labels in the 3D data do not follow handedness: a handedness table agrees with the faster wrist in only 61% of serves, probably because half of the serves are seen mirrored.

Features:

Motion features are computed from hip-centred, low-pass-filtered (8 Hz) joint positions.
Shape features are median bone lengths. The 3D lifting largely normalises skeleton size (bone lengths barely relate to player height, r ≤ 0.23), so shape describes proportions rather than size.

Evaluation:

Gender, serve number and serve speed: repeated player-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds).
Player identity: repeated match-level cross-validation (3 repetitions of 5 folds), on the 46 eligible players.
Random Forest: 300 trees, maximum depth 10, at least 5 samples per leaf.
Metrics: balanced accuracy and AUC (gender, serve number), balanced accuracy and top-1 accuracy (identity), MAE in km/h (serve speed).

Uncertainty:

95% confidence intervals come from a player-level bootstrap (2,000 resamples of the players). For identity, players are resampled as classes.
Comparisons between models use paired bootstrap differences on the same resampled players.
A confidence interval containing zero means there is not enough evidence of a clear difference, not proof that two models are equivalent.
